Rather than releasing a human-written paper, Alpöge published a massive, machine-verified proof coded in the Lean programming language. Because the AI's raw natural-language output stretched to roughly 250,000 pages—far too long for humans to read—Alpöge and Claude translated the entire proof into 17 million lines of Lean code. Lean acts as an automated proof assistant that mechanically checks every single logical step against foundational mathematical axioms. The Mathematical Strategy  In Alpöge's four-page summary, he outlined the core mathematical techniques Claude used to crack the 167-year-old problem: • Adapting Function Field Methods. The proof draws inspiration from Stepanov's method, which originally proved the Riemann Hypothesis in simpler "function field" settings using derivatives and multiplicity. Because you cannot take standard derivatives of whole numbers, translating this approach to the integers has historically been a major roadblock. • Fourier Analysis and Theta Lifts. To bypass the integer roadblock, Alpöge and Claude combined new Fourier-analytic ideas with chaining theta lifts. This allowed the proof's underlying tricks to recur in a natively automorphic way, constructing specialized lattice theta sums to tightly bound the error term in counting prime numbers. • Broader Scope. According to Alpöge, the method does not just resolve the classical Riemann Hypothesis; it also proves the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions.
